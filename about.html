KLEPTO Play

About KLEPTO

A small droid in a tower full of bigger ones. It can't win a fair fight, so it steals.

What it is

KLEPTO is a remake, of sorts, of Quazatron, which Steve Turner wrote for the ZX Spectrum and Hewson published in 1986. It was itself a Spectrum take on Andrew Braybrook's Paradroid. In both you play a weak droid that can latch on to other droids, beat them in a circuit-board duel and take something from them. In Quazatron that something was their parts.

I wanted to keep that idea, the isometric platforms and the duel, and build a deeper game around them. The droid you play is still called KLP-2, which is where the name comes from.

How a run works

  • Five decks and then the Mainframe. Each deck is stitched together from hand-made rooms, so the layout changes every run but every room was designed.
  • KLP-2 starts with wheels and a bumper. It has three sockets, for a drive, an arm and a core, and there are nine parts to steal: thrusters, hover, treads, a laser, an arc coil, a tractor beam, a shield, a cloak and a phase core.
  • Every droid is built from the parts it carries, and it fights with them. What you see on a droid is what you can take from it. A part's grade comes from the class number of the droid you took it from.
  • Beat a droid in a hack and you choose: take a part, turn it cyan so it joins your gang, or strip it for scrap. Scrap also bursts out of anything you wreck, and buys repairs, upgrades and firmware at a fabricator.
  • One droid on each deck is a bounty, with a name and parts better than its class.
  • Some rooms are sealed vaults that need a particular part: thrusters for a gap, treads for a heavy crate, phase for a force field. The way down never needs one, so a run can't jam.
  • Each deck's lift is locked until you take its command droid, by hack or by force.
  • When KLP-2 is destroyed the run ends. The droid archive stays, and any part you have ever stolen can come with you, at grade I, on the next run.
  • Your first run starts with the induction, a hand-built first deck with signs on the floor. Today's tower gives everyone the same five decks for the day.

The hack

Grapple a droid and you're dropped into its circuits. Two sides of a board fight over a column of cells in the middle. You pick the side whose wires look better, then fire pulses down them. A powered cell turns your colour. Own more cells when the timer runs out and the droid is yours.

The wires carry parts that change the sums: forks that reach two cells, AND gates that need two pulses at once, inverts that paint your opponent's colour, locks that stay lit, and the odd free wire. Damage a droid before you grapple it and it fights back with fewer pulsers. Stun it and it starts late. Grapple it from a cloak and you ambush it.

You don't meet all of that at once. The first hack is four plain wires against a slow opponent. Each one you win brings in one new part, on a small board built around it, with a line saying what it does.

The colours

Quazatron on the Spectrum was mostly one colour on black, because the machine could only manage two colours in any 8 by 8 block of pixels. KLEPTO borrows that idea rather than the look. Each deck takes one ink from the Spectrum's palette as its hostile colour. KLP-2 is always cyan. As you hack and wreck the deck's machines, its light drifts from the hostile colour to cyan, so you can see how much of it you've claimed.

There is also a 1986 mode in the settings, which draws the game the way the Spectrum would have had to: 192 lines, black paper and one bright ink in each 8 by 8 block. Where KLP-2's cyan meets a magenta wall, one of them has to give. That's attribute clash.

How it's made

I don't write code. I described what I wanted to Claude, Anthropic's AI model, working in Claude Code, and it did the typing, the testing and a good deal of the design. My part was the brief, the decisions and the fussing.

  • It's built with three.js in plain JavaScript, with no build step. It runs as static files.
  • There are no image files in the game. Floors, walls and displays are drawn on canvases when it loads; droids are put together from simple shapes.
  • Every sound is made in the browser with the Web Audio API, including a small score for each deck that gets busier when you're in a fight.
  • The fonts are Chakra Petch and IBM Plex, both under the Open Font License, served from this site.
  • It's desktop only, played with a keyboard or a gamepad. The movement and the timed hack both want quick, precise input, and touch controls would have been a poor copy. A controller paired to a phone will work.
  • The menus are real buttons, so they work by keyboard, gamepad and screen reader, and the game announces what happens. It follows your system's reduced motion setting, and there are options to take less damage and slow the hack timer. The action itself still needs sight and quick hands, and I won't pretend otherwise.
  • Your settings, the archive and your records are kept in your browser and go nowhere else.

29 October 2026

A proper slope for the hack

My first hack after the induction was a mess: eleven parts on my side of the board, seven of them AND gates. Claude measured the boards and found the lesson boards were to blame. They were meant to be built around the new part and had ended up buried in it, busier than the first ordinary board after the lessons.

A lesson board now carries the new part two or three times, with four or five parts in all. Ordinary boards have a budget that rises with the droid's class: about four parts on a Mite, six or seven by class 300, nine by class 400, and fewer AND gates on the small ones. From class 500 the boards are left as they were, so the end of a run is as hard as before. Small droids are also a little slower in the hack.

Damage always counted, but nothing showed it. Ram a droid before you grapple it and the board now says so, "Dented: 1 pulser down", with the lost pulser crossed out, and "Stunned: starts late" if you've stunned it. The Spark room in the induction has a new sign: ram it, then hack it.

And wire hints. While you're learning, the arrow at the start of each wire is coloured by where it lands: your colour, theirs, or grey for nowhere, with an & where a wire needs a partner. A setting keeps them on for good.

Later the same day I asked whether the hack shows how many shots you have left. It did, barely: four tiny dots that only appeared once the hack began. They're bigger now, with the number written beside them, there from the moment the board appears, and they flash if you try to fire with none left.

29 October 2026

Colour doing more work in the hack

My suggestion: the invert should show what it does. Its ring is now split down the middle, cyan on the side your pulse comes in and their colour on the side it goes out, so on their side of the board it's the other way round.

Claude added two more. The wire you've selected now has a faint trail along it, in the colour it would paint, so you can follow a pulse through the forks before you fire, and watch it turn their colour at an invert. And an AND gate has a small light on each input, so you can see when it has one pulse and is waiting for the other. The trail stops at a hidden part, which also fixes an old leak: the dotted preview used to give away what was hiding there.

The fabricator also shows how much scrap you have, in a proper counter rather than a line of grey text.

29 October 2026 Milestone

A gentler start, and more fun

I played it properly and found the start overwhelming. The first hack threw everything at you: eight wires, five kinds of part, a side to pick against the clock, and four pages of instructions before you'd pressed a key.

Now the hack teaches itself. The first board has four plain wires and an opponent in no hurry. Win it and the next board brings in forks, then cut wires and choosing a side, then inverts, AND gates, locks and free wires, one at a time, each on a small board built around the new part with a single line about it. The pages of instructions have gone. Hacks on small droids are a little quicker, too.

The first deck of your first run is now built by hand: the induction. Seven rooms, each bringing in one thing with a sign painted on the floor. A Mite asleep on the job to practise the hack on, a Spark with a laser to steal, a target across a gap to try it on, a ledge with three Mites on it to ram off, a terminal that opens a door, and the command droid guarding the lift. You can skip it, and replay it from the title screen. After it the run carries on with generated decks as before.

Then I asked for it to be a lot more fun, which is harder to specify. The first answer is scrap. Wrecked droids burst into it, and KLP-2 hoovers it up as it rolls past. Knock a droid off an edge and the game slows down to watch it fall, shouts RING OUT and pays double. Each deck's lift room has a fabricator where scrap buys repairs, better grades of the parts you already have, and firmware. Win a hack by four cells or more and you can take two parts instead of one.

The second answer is a gang. Win a hack and, instead of taking a part, you can turn the droid. It goes cyan, follows you about and fights for you, and it rides the lift down with you. Three at most. A turned Mite, which spent its life running away from you, now runs at other droids, and I find that more pleasing than I can justify.

The third is personality. Droids talk now, in short capital letters over their heads: they spot you, complain when you dent them and get a last word on the way into the void. Mites squeak and run. Your gang passes the odd remark on the way round.

And it's quicker all round. KLP-2 is faster than the droids now, so you can catch a Mite and line up a ram. Decks are a room or so shorter, and the grapple snaps on faster.

Each deck also has a bounty: one droid with a name, a brass shell and parts two grades better than it ought to have. Bishop, Lady Volt, Sump King. It announces itself when it sees you. Hack it for those parts, or wreck it, and either way you get a pile of scrap and a firmware chip.

Finally, an idea Claude pitched that I liked: today's tower. Everyone who plays on the same day gets the same five decks, and the first go is the one that counts. At the end you get a line of coloured squares to share, one per deck, with a bang where KLP-2 went down. I'm aware of where that idea comes from.

29 October 2026

More rooms, and a second pair of eyes

Thirteen more rooms, so a run doesn't keep showing you the same ten. Each deck now leans towards rooms of its own: Freight gets a loading bay and a sump full of crates, Reactor gets coolant channels of live floor, Security gets a watchtower and a row of cells behind force fields.

Then a separate Claude reviewed the code cold, without being told how any of it was meant to work. It found eleven bugs. The worst: starting a new run after dying handed you back the dead droid, with one point of integrity. All fixed, and the fixes checked in a browser.

Smaller things from the same round: you can pause a hack, the camera zooms on + and -, droids turn their visors to watch you, a controller rumbles when you hit something, and winning a hack pulls a stream of light out of the machine and into KLP-2.

29 October 2026 Milestone

First playable run

The first version that goes from the title screen to the Mainframe and back. Five decks plus the finale, fifteen families of droid, nine parts, firmware upgrades, vaults, an archive that lasts between runs, and the hack.

The hack got the most attention, since it's the heart of the thing. The original's boards were fun but opaque, so this one shows you which cells a wire will reach before you fire, and each cell carries a small arrow pointing at its owner so the board still reads without colour. A headless test pits a decent player against droids of every class: the cheap ones lose more than eight times in ten, the officers on deck 5 still win about three times in ten.

Before calling it playable, Claude wrote a bot that plays whole runs with no graphics, at speed. It found that about one deck in eleven had a lift you couldn't reach, and that KLP-2 rolled off edges far too easily. Both are fixed. Every change now gets checked against 1,800 generated decks, and KLP-2 won't drive itself off a ledge, though a ram can still put it over.

Droids came out better than I expected: little enamel domes with a lit visor and their class number on the front, like the originals, but carrying whatever they've got bolted on. A Courier has thrusters on its back. A Warden has shield fins. You learn what a part does by being on the wrong end of it first.

29 October 2026

The brief, and three decisions

I asked for Quazatron, much improved: the isometric movement and the grappling kept, the rest made deeper and better looking, with powers you take from the machines you hack and puzzles that need the right ones.

A roguelite. Halfway through the brief it struck me that this could be a roguelite, and it fits unusually well, because the game was always about starting weak and stealing your way up. The risk with procedural levels is a puzzle you can't solve with what you've got, so the puzzles moved into optional vaults, and the generator makes sure the part that opens each vault is walking around somewhere on the same deck.

Desktop only. I worried it wouldn't work on a phone, and it doesn't really. Better to say so plainly than ship a cramped virtual joystick.

KLEPTO. Quazatron is still someone else's name, so this one is named after its hero.
